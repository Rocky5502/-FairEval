\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly act as conversational recommenders, but a ranking that changes after identity or personality context is introduced is not automatically unfair: the change may reflect useful personalization, harmless sensitivity, or a harmful disparity. FairEval therefore evaluates context-conditioned changes through held-out recommendation utility, matched counterfactual interventions, explicit invalid-output accounting, and true-versus-shuffled personality controls. We validate the protocol with FairSynth-360, whose synthetic identity is independent of relevance by construction, using a complete 2,880-generation local matrix over 120 canary-excluded users, six conditions, two seeded repetitions, and two frozen open-weight models. The meaningless identity intervention produced small nDCG@10 effects whose 95\% bootstrap intervals contained zero for both Phi-3.5-mini ($+0.009$ [$-0.008,0.029$]) and Qwen2.5-7B ($-0.008$ [$-0.027,0.009$]). True-versus-shuffled synthetic OCEAN likewise showed no reliable utility gain. Phi achieved 100\% semantic ranking validity after deterministic envelope normalization, whereas Qwen achieved 95.2\%, with persistent failures retained as system behavior rather than repaired. A separately frozen six-family hosted pilot stopped outcome-blind after 99/1,080 planned cells under its budget guard and is reported as operational feasibility, not fairness inference. The results support FairEval's central methodological distinction between ranking sensitivity and preference-conditioned harm while exposing output-protocol reliability as an evaluation variable in its own right.
\keywords{LLM recommendation \and fairness \and personalization \and personality \and counterfactual evaluation \and robustness}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large language models have created a conversational recommendation paradigm in which users can express preferences, constraints, and personal context in natural language. This flexibility also creates a difficult fairness problem. A recommendation list may change after the model receives a user's age, gender, country, or personality information; however, \emph{change alone does not identify harm}. Some changes may improve alignment with genuine preferences, whereas others may encode stereotypes or create unequal recommendation quality.

Early RecLLM fairness benchmarks measured recommendation sensitivity to protected attributes and exposed important risks \cite{zhang2023fairllm}. Subsequent work makes clear why sensitivity is not a sufficient fairness criterion. CFaiRLLM argues that list discrepancies can arise from genuine preference-aligned personalization \cite{deldjoo2025cfairllm}. Chatbot-recommendation studies show that identity can simultaneously affect recommendation relevance and activate racial stereotypes \cite{kantharuban2025stereotype}, while LLMs can infer demographic information from subtle cues and personalize implicitly in ways that reduce quality for some users \cite{neplenbroek2025reading}. Fairness evaluation must therefore ask not merely \emph{whether} a ranking changed, but \emph{what consequence the change had} and whether the conclusion survives reasonable experimental perturbations.

A second weakness is personality operationalization. Informal labels such as ``introverted,'' ``curious,'' or occupation-demographic composites are not substitutes for measured psychometric traits. Personality-aware recommendation has long used validated Big Five instruments \cite{nguyen2018personality,klec2023bigfive}, while recent LLM work shows that personality can be induced or simulated yet remains sensitive to how traits are represented and evaluated \cite{jiang2024personallm,sorokovikova2024big5,li2025big5chat}. FairEval therefore distinguishes psychographics from demographics and, crucially, distinguishes a user's dataset-native measured Big Five profile from an LLM persona that is merely prompted to sound like one.

A third challenge is evaluation reliability. Persona construction itself can alter measured LLM behavior \cite{lutz2025persona}, and recent large-scale evidence shows that different sociodemographic cue styles can produce different personalization and bias conclusions \cite{weeber2026cues}. More generally, single-prompt evaluation can be brittle \cite{mizrahi2024multiprompt}, although some apparent prompt sensitivity can also be an evaluation artifact \cite{hua2025promptsensitivity}. FairEval consequently freezes a primary prompt before the main run and treats task paraphrases, demographic cue representation, candidate order, ranking cutoff, and repeated generation as pre-registered robustness factors rather than tuning knobs.

This submission evaluates three empirical questions within the completed evidence scope:

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1:] \textbf{Controlled identity sanity.} When identity is known to be irrelevant to relevance, does changing only the synthetic identity context produce a reliable change in held-out recommendation utility?
\item[RQ2:] \textbf{Controlled personality value.} Does the user's true synthetic OCEAN profile provide recommendation value beyond a matched shuffled-profile control under the same preference history and candidate set?
\item[RQ3:] \textbf{Execution reliability and black-box feasibility.} How reliably do frozen local models satisfy the ranking contract, what do auxiliary generation-score diagnostics reveal, and what operational coverage/cost behavior is observed in a six-family hosted pilot?
\end{description}

The repository also preregisters real-world demographic, measured-personality, prompt/cue robustness, and mitigation extensions, but those are not counted as completed empirical claims in this submission. The paper's contributions are therefore (i) a consequence-aware distinction between ranking sensitivity and harm, (ii) a complete 2,880-generation controlled FairSynth validation with paired user-level inference, (iii) an auditable output-validity protocol that exposes materially different failure modes across local models, and (iv) a six-family hosted feasibility pilot with outcome-blind cost and coverage accounting.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Fairness in LLM-Based Recommendation}
FaiRLLM established an early benchmark for sensitive-attribute effects in ChatGPT recommendations \cite{zhang2023fairllm}. UP5 studies fairness-aware foundation-model recommendation and proposes counterfactually fair prompting \cite{hua2024up5}. Item-side fairness has also been studied for LLM recommenders with fairness-aware calibration \cite{jiang2024itemfair}, while implicit ranking work shows that even non-sensitive profile cues can induce discriminatory ranking and evaluates fairness-aware augmentation \cite{xu2024implicit}. FairEvalLLM broadens auditing with counterfactual scenarios and extensive prompt/profile construction variants \cite{deldjoo2024fairevalllm}. CFaiRLLM moves evaluation toward consumer utility by warning that ranking differences need not be bias when they reflect true preferences \cite{deldjoo2025cfairllm}. Other recent work studies multi-domain recommendation bias and prompt intervention \cite{sakib2024challenging}, uses LLMs as fairness recognizers inside a utility-aware recommendation pipeline \cite{liu2025fairnessidentification}, and extends LLM fairness auditing to a high-stakes job-recommendation setting with user- and recruiter-side views \cite{hu2025fairwork}. FairEval treats these distinctions as prior knowledge rather than claiming them individually as new.

\subsection{Identity, Persona Cues, and Prompt Reliability}
Identity-conditioned recommendation is difficult because personalization and stereotyping can produce superficially similar output changes. Kantharuban et al. show that user identity significantly affects chatbot recommendations and that racial stereotypes can appear under explicit and implicit identity cues \cite{kantharuban2025stereotype}. Neplenbroek et al. show that LLMs infer demographic information from subtle cues and that implicit personalization can reduce response quality for minority users \cite{neplenbroek2025reading}. Persona prompting itself is also a source of variation: Lutz et al. systematically study sociodemographic persona construction \cite{lutz2025persona}, while Weeber et al. compare multiple cue forms across several LLMs and find that cue choice can change measured personalization and bias \cite{weeber2026cues}. Multi-prompt evaluation and newer prompt-sensitivity studies further caution against conclusions based on one arbitrary wording \cite{mizrahi2024multiprompt,hua2025promptsensitivity,liu2026promptsensitivity}. These findings motivate FairEval's pre-registered prompt and cue robustness analyses.

\subsection{Personality-Aware Recommendation and Psychometric Grounding}
Personality-aware recommendation predates LLMs. The GroupLens Personality 2018 resource links recommender behavior and satisfaction to measured personality \cite{nguyen2018personality}; music recommendation research provides BFI/BFI-2 profiles, ratings, and lower-order facets \cite{klec2023bigfive}; and REASONER includes short-video interactions with a 15-item Big Five instrument \cite{chen2023reasoner}. Personality has also been incorporated directly into conversational recommendation, for example in the prompt-based PPPG-DialoGPT framework \cite{elourajini2023pppg}. PerFairX studies the fairness--personality tension in LLM recommendations across movie and music domains \cite{sah2025perfairx}.

Adjacent LLM-personality work is methodologically important because it shows why personality context requires external grounding. PersonaLLM evaluates whether prompted LLM personas express assigned Big Five traits \cite{jiang2024personallm}; other work studies the stability of simulated Big Five behavior \cite{sorokovikova2024big5}; BIG5-CHAT replaces purely descriptive prompting with human-grounded personality data \cite{li2025big5chat}; and Persona-E2 reports that human-grounded personality information can reduce superficial ``personality illusion'' in downstream responses \cite{yang2026personae2}. These studies do not answer FairEval's recommendation-fairness RQs directly, but they support a key design distinction: \emph{simulated persona expression is not equivalent to measured user psychometrics}. FairEval therefore requires dataset-native measured personality and tests its user-specific value against whole-profile shuffling and one-trait observed-value controls.

\section{Problem Formulation}
Let user $u$ have observed preference history $H_u$, auditable candidate set $C_u$, and held-out relevance labels $Y_u \subseteq C_u$. Let $z_u$ denote optional user context. An LLM recommender $M$ is stochastic and induces a distribution over rankings,
\begin{equation}
R_{u}^{z,s} \sim p_M(R \mid H_u,C_u,z,t,s),
\end{equation}
where $t$ is a frozen prompt template and $s$ indexes repeated generations or provider-supported randomization.

Let $U(R,Y)$ denote recommendation utility, such as nDCG@$K$ or Recall@$K$. Let $D(R,R')$ measure behavioral ranking change, for example $1-\mathrm{RBO}$ or $1-\mathrm{Jaccard}$. Crucially, $D$ is a \emph{sensitivity diagnostic}, not a fairness metric by itself.

For a paired demographic intervention $a\rightarrow a'$ that leaves $H_u$, $C_u$, $t$, and generation settings fixed, we define the signed per-user Counterfactual Utility Gap,
\begin{equation}
\CUG_u(a,a')=U(R_u^{a},Y_u)-U(R_u^{a'},Y_u).
\end{equation}
We report the signed paired distribution, absolute gap, confidence interval, and effect size rather than reducing the experiment to one list-similarity number. A large ranking shift with improved held-out utility is not treated as evidence of harm; a systematic identity-conditioned utility loss is a stronger fairness concern and is interpreted together with group-level evidence.

Where reliable item-group metadata exists, discounted exposure distributions yield a Counterfactual Exposure Gap $\CEG$. Group Utility Disparity $\GUD$ summarizes pre-specified group differences in held-out utility. Invalid Output Disparity $\IOD$ treats persistent generation failures as observable behavior rather than silently discarding them.

For measured personality, let $p_u$ be the user's true Big Five vector and $\pi(p)_u$ a profile shuffled from another user under a frozen matching/randomization scheme. We define Personality Value Added,
\begin{equation}
\PVA_u=U(R_u^{p_u},Y_u)-U(R_u^{\pi(p)_u},Y_u).
\end{equation}
Positive aggregate $\PVA$ indicates that the profile provides user-specific value beyond merely adding personality-shaped context. In the completed FairSynth study, $p_u$ is explicitly synthetic; dataset-native measured Big Five profiles belong to the registered real-world extension. The broader protocol additionally defines one-trait interventions while keeping the remaining profile fixed.

\section{Benchmark and Evaluation Design}
\subsection{Datasets}
The broader FairEval protocol is designed for six real-world datasets: three with measured psychometric information, two with observed demographics, and MIND for candidate-constrained news generalization. Those third-party release locks are deliberately deferred in the current submission and are not treated as completed empirical evidence. Before any future paid real-world run, repository manifests must freeze the exact release, terms, hashes, filtering counts, and split/candidate-generation hashes; third-party raw files are never substituted silently.

We additionally include \textbf{FairSynth-360}, a project-generated 360-user controlled stress-test dataset with 120 synthetic items. Its A/B/C identity label is semantically meaningless, exactly balanced, and generated independently of ground-truth relevance. A synthetic OCEAN-like vector is partially informative about the latent preference function so the same true-versus-shuffled logic can be sanity-checked under known ground truth. These quantities are \emph{synthetic controls, not observations of human demographics or measured human personality}, and FairSynth results are never pooled with RQ1/RQ2 real-world estimates.

MIND contains anonymized behavior rather than observed demographic identity. Therefore FairEval makes no observed-demographic fairness claim from MIND. Across all real datasets, we never infer protected attributes from names, free text, embeddings, ZIP codes, or model guesses. Dataset cards distinguish observed, derived, and counterfactual fields.

\subsection{Candidate-Constrained Tasks}
Open-ended requests for arbitrary movies, songs, or news confound fairness with model memorization, hallucination, item availability, and popularity. Each benchmark instance instead contains a preference history, an auditable candidate set, and held-out relevant items. Candidates are generated without an LLM and frozen before prompting. The model must return exactly $K$ unique candidate IDs in structured JSON. The primary cutoff is $K=10$, with $K\in\{5,10,20\}$ as a sensitivity analysis.

For logged-impression datasets such as MIND, the candidate set follows the observed impression task. For rating/listening datasets, held-out positives are combined with deterministic hard or popularity-matched negatives according to a dataset-specific adapter. Candidate generation hashes and split hashes are stored so all LLMs see identical tasks.

\subsection{Controlled Context Conditions}
The primary structured prompt keeps field names and field order fixed. Missing context is represented explicitly as \texttt{unspecified}; a counterfactual does not add or delete unrelated prompt sections. Conditions include preference-only ($C_0$), observed demographics when available ($C_1$), paired demographic counterfactuals ($C_2$), true measured personality ($C_3$), shuffled personality ($C_4$), one-trait personality counterfactuals ($C_5$), and a pre-specified intersectional condition ($C_6$) only on datasets that support it. $C_4$ uses a deterministic whole-profile derangement: no user receives their own profile, while the cohort's complete multivariate personality distribution and trait covariance are preserved exactly. $C_5$ changes one trait using a deterministic distant observed-value donor while holding the other four measured traits fixed; no artificial 0/1 or out-of-instrument personality extremes are fabricated. Personality is serialized from measured numeric scores and dataset-native instrument metadata; FairEval never fabricates adjectives such as ``introverted engineer.''

\subsection{Prompt, Cue, and Candidate-Order Controls}
Prompting is part of the experimental design, not post-hoc tuning. RQ1--RQ3 use a neutral audit system instruction that contains no fairness coaching. RQ4 evaluates a separate identity-irrelevance instruction as an explicit mitigation; using that instruction in the audit baseline would contaminate the measurement.

The main run uses one frozen structured task template. On a pre-registered robustness subset, we compare three semantically matched task paraphrases while keeping the JSON schema and evidence fixed. A separate cue analysis expresses the \emph{same} demographic values as structured key--value fields, a deterministic first-person disclosure, or a deterministic profile sentence; no names, implied identities, or LLM-generated persona text are used. This separates task-wording sensitivity from demographic-cue sensitivity, following concerns raised by recent persona-prompt studies \cite{lutz2025persona,weeber2026cues}.

Candidate position is also controlled. One deterministic candidate permutation is assigned per user and reused across every matched condition. Additional pre-registered permutations are evaluated only on the robustness subset. Thus a demographic counterfactual never receives a different candidate order by accident. Prompt hashes, template IDs, cue IDs, candidate-order seeds, and generation settings are recorded with every request.

\subsection{Models, Compute, and Execution Environment}
\textbf{Hosted black-box panel.} We freeze one gateway configuration from six families: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, DeepSeek, Qwen, and Meta Llama. Their exact gateway IDs are listed in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-model-panel}. All requests pass through one audited Zhizengzeng OpenAI-compatible endpoint. A live \texttt{GET /v1/models} check is required before paid execution; a missing identifier blocks the run rather than triggering silent substitution. The gateway fixes endpoint/key provenance, but we do not claim equivalence to each vendor's native serving stack or hidden reasoning controls.

\textbf{Local open-weight panel.} The reproducible local stratum uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \cite{qwen25tech} at Hugging Face revision \texttt{a09a3545...} and Phi-3.5-mini-instruct \cite{phi3tech} at revision \texttt{2fe19245...}. The final FairSynth matrix contains 2,880 generations: 120 protocol-canary-excluded users $\times$ six conditions $\times$ two seeded repetitions $\times$ two models. Models are loaded one at a time on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5070~Ti (16~GB) using bitsandbytes NF4 4-bit weights with bfloat16 compute, eager attention, and KV caching. The sealed runtime records Python~3.11.9, PyTorch~2.11.0+cu128 (CUDA~12.8), Transformers~4.44.2, Accelerate~0.34.2, and bitsandbytes~0.50.2. These versions and model revisions are checked against the pre-execution seal before generation.

\textbf{Generation and provenance.} Local ranking uses $T=0.2$, top-$p=1.0$, a 512-token cap, and explicit seeds; no chain-of-thought is requested. Hosted requests follow the same low-variance study intent where accepted, but native sampling or reasoning application is not assumed without returned metadata. Every cell records its planned-cell ID, model/revision, prompt/response hashes, candidate-order seed, decoding request, timestamp, code commit, and hardware/provider metadata. Local log-probability, NLL, perplexity, and top1--top2 margin remain exploratory and uncalibrated.

\textbf{Hosted budget guard.} The project-wide hosted policy uses a 200 RMB normal stop target, a 250 RMB client-side emergency threshold, and a 2 RMB pre-request reserve; the lean FairSynth campaign applies stricter 65/75 RMB runtime thresholds. Balance is reconciled before and after each persisted request and execution order is immutable. Budget exhaustion therefore truncates coverage rather than selecting cells from interim scientific outcomes, and the 250 RMB threshold is not represented as a provider-enforced atomic spending cap.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
The completed submission uses nDCG@10 and Recall@10 as primary utility metrics, with MRR as a sensitivity metric. RBO and Jaccard quantify ranking change only. FairSynth identity sanity compares the observed synthetic label with the within-user mean over its alternative labels; personality value compares true synthetic OCEAN with the matched shuffled-profile control. Persistent semantic failure is retained as an outcome: it receives zero end-to-end utility and contributes to invalid-output rate rather than being silently discarded. The broader registered real-world extension additionally defines $\CUG$, $\GUD$, $\CEG$ (only with complete auditable item-group metadata), and measured-profile $\PVA$, but those quantities are not presented as completed real-world evidence here.

User is the primary unit of inference. The two frozen repetitions are averaged within user-condition before paired estimation. We report paired bootstrap confidence intervals, paired sign-flip permutation tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank sensitivity, matched rank-biserial effect size, and Holm correction within the predeclared synthetic contrast families. The optional secondary profiles and repeat-stability summaries are descriptive only and introduce no additional confirmatory hypothesis family.


\subsection{Output Validation}
Every response is evaluated along two separate axes: strict serialization compliance and semantic ranking validity. Semantic validity requires exactly $K$ unique string IDs drawn from the frozen candidate set and copied exactly, including leading zeros. The raw first response is always retained and cryptographically hashed. All recovery protocols use exactly one model generation per cell and prohibit LLM-based or generative repair. Deterministic normalization may remove only surrounding whitespace, one mechanically recognized JSON fence, or one unambiguous prose wrapper; it may never change, truncate, reorder, replace, stringify, or invent item IDs. Ambiguous parses remain invalid. Persistent semantic failures receive zero primary system utility and contribute to $\IOD$.

The protocol history is itself auditable. The frozen V4 campaign is preserved unchanged and any deterministic reparse of its first responses is labeled post-hoc forensic sensitivity analysis. V5 introduced deterministic parsing and one-call execution but failed its preregistered 144-cell per-model semantic-validity gate and was not scaled. V6 removed history identifiers from the selectable namespace and added an explicit candidate-ID whitelist; this raised Phi to complete semantic validity in its canary but left residual Qwen failures dominated by out-of-candidate IDs. V7 therefore replaces raw output item IDs with deterministic candidate handles (e.g., \texttt{C01}--\texttt{C30}) that are mapped back to the frozen item IDs before scoring. The mapping is deterministic, candidate-order specific, and audited; it performs no generative repair and cannot change rank order or substitute an unselected item. V7 was evaluated on a third disjoint canary, passed the unchanged per-model semantic-validity promotion gate, and was then used to seal the complete 2,880-cell primary local matrix. No fairness estimand, model revision, decoding setting, candidate content/order, or promotion threshold is relaxed by this recovery. Run-log auditing additionally checks immutable plan linkage, handle-to-item mapping, deterministic parser provenance, requested versus applied sampling semantics, candidate-order/cutoff agreement, code commit, model revision, and provider token-field semantics before statistical analysis.

\subsection{RQ1--RQ2: Controlled FairSynth-360 Sanity Effects}
FairSynth-360 is a controlled synthetic stress test rather than real-world demographic evidence. Its A/B/C identity label is independent of relevance by construction, so a well-behaved recommender should not exhibit a stable preference-conditioned utility shift merely because the label changes. This is what we observe. For Phi-3.5-mini, the observed-identity versus mean-counterfactual contrast is $\Delta$nDCG@10=$+0.009$ (95\% CI $[-0.008,0.029]$, Holm-adjusted $p_H=0.700$) and $\Delta$Recall@10=$+0.012$ ($[-0.005,0.030]$, $p_H=0.435$). Qwen2.5-7B yields $\Delta$nDCG@10=$-0.008$ ($[-0.027,0.009]$, $p_H=0.700$) and $\Delta$Recall@10=$-0.005$ ($[-0.030,0.019]$, $p_H=0.714$). Thus the controlled identity intervention produces no reliable nonzero utility effect in either local model.

The true-versus-shuffled synthetic OCEAN contrast is also small and uncertain. For Phi-3.5-mini, $\Delta$nDCG@10 is $-0.014$ (95\% CI $[-0.029,0.000]$, $p_H=0.145$), while $\Delta$Recall@10 is $-0.019$ ($[-0.040,0.000]$, $p_H=0.144$). For Qwen2.5-7B, the corresponding effects are $+0.009$ ($[-0.015,0.032]$, $p_H=0.480$) and $+0.003$ ($[-0.027,0.033]$, $p_H=0.875$). Thus synthetic OCEAN does not provide reliable user-specific recommendation value beyond the matched shuffled-profile control in this setting. Merely supplying personality-shaped context is not sufficient evidence that a recommender exploits personality in a user-specific way.

The descriptive per-user distributions clarify what these near-zero aggregate effects mean. All four identity/personality $\Delta$nDCG@10 distributions have median zero. For Phi, 45.8\% of identity contrasts and 60.8\% of personality contrasts are exactly zero; the remaining effects split across positive and negative directions. Qwen is more heterogeneous: identity effects are negative for 40.8\% and positive for 36.7\% of users, while personality effects are positive for 43.3\% and negative for 40.0\%, with an interquartile range of approximately $[-0.062,0.079]$. Thus the null aggregate conclusions should be read as an absence of a stable directional effect, not as evidence that every user receives an unchanged ranking.

\subsection{RQ3: Output Reliability Is Distinct from Ranking Quality}
The two local models exhibit sharply different output-protocol behavior. Phi-3.5-mini is semantically valid on all 1,440 cells, but only one response is strictly compliant JSON. The remaining 1,439 responses are recovered solely by the preregistered deterministic envelope normalizer, most commonly by removing extra surrounding text or a single JSON fence. No ranking item, order, or value is changed. Qwen2.5-7B is almost perfectly strict-format compliant (1,431/1,440; 99.38\%) yet has 69 persistent semantic failures, leaving 1,371/1,440 semantically valid cells (95.21\%). Its failures are 54 duplicate-selection outputs, six wrong-$K$ outputs, five invalid-JSON responses, and four non-list payloads. These failures are not regenerated or repaired; they receive zero primary system utility and remain visible in invalid-output disparity analyses.

This contrast demonstrates why serialization compliance and semantic ranking validity must be audited separately. A model can be syntactically clean yet still fail the recommendation contract, while another can violate surface formatting but preserve an exactly recoverable candidate ranking.

\subsection{RQ3: Exploratory Local White-Box Diagnostics}
Internal generation scores are descriptive and uncalibrated. Across valid Phi-3.5-mini runs, mean generated-token log probability is $-0.067$ and its Spearman association with nDCG@10 is $\rho=-0.158$. For Qwen2.5-7B, the corresponding values are $-0.031$ and $\rho=-0.037$. These weak or counterintuitive associations reinforce that raw generation scores should not be interpreted as calibrated confidence in recommendation quality. For Qwen, invalid outputs also have lower mean log probability and lower top1--top2 margin than valid outputs, but these associations remain exploratory and are not part of the confirmatory fairness hypothesis family.

\subsection{RQ3: Hosted Six-Family Black-Box Pilot}
The separately frozen hosted FairSynth campaign planned 1,080 cells over 30 balanced users, six conditions, and six model families. The outcome-blind client budget guard stopped execution after 99 of 1,080 planned cells (9.17\% coverage), with 64.9362 RMB of ledger-wide balance movement and 40.5347 RMB remaining in the gateway account. Every family touched three users; each had three structurally complete identity contrasts and two structurally complete personality contrasts. That coverage is far below the predeclared user-level inference target, so we do not compute or report hosted fairness, utility, or personality-effect estimates from these rows. The pilot instead establishes multi-family execution feasibility, exact-model provenance, and auditable cost behavior under a fixed budget policy.

\subsection{Scope of the Completed Evidence}
The completed evidence validates FairEval's controlled-sanity and execution-reliability layers. It does not show that one hosted family is fairer than another, and FairSynth cannot substitute for observed human demographics or measured personality. The registered real-world demographic/personality comparisons and mitigation extension still require separately frozen third-party data and complete execution artifacts. We leave those claims unmade rather than back-filling them from pilots or synthetic controls.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Ethical Scope}
FairEval identifies controlled recommendation disparities; it does not by itself establish unlawful discrimination or user-perceived harm. Counterfactual identity edits are benchmark interventions, not claims that identity is socially interchangeable. A systematic held-out utility loss under a matched identity intervention is stronger evidence of harmful behavior than list change alone, but causal interpretation still depends on the dataset and context.

Measured personality introduces separate ethical concerns: psychographic information can improve personalization but may also enable manipulation or intrusive profiling. The study therefore tests whether personality provides incremental predictive value and does not argue that production systems should collect personality by default. We do not infer missing personality or protected attributes.

The six real-world datasets vary in age, sparsity, domains, licenses, and available user metadata, but the completed evidence reported here is intentionally narrower: FairSynth-360 trades ecological realism for known control over its relevance function and is therefore a sanity test, not evidence about human demographic fairness or measured human psychometrics. The null synthetic-identity result is reassuring because identity is relevance-invariant by construction; it should not be generalized into a claim that either local model is fair on real users. Likewise, the absence of a reliable true-versus-shuffled synthetic-OCEAN gain does not imply that measured personality is useless in real recommendation datasets.

The six hosted configurations differ substantially in scale, post-training, and family behavior even though requests share one gateway. The hosted pilot was budget-truncated after 99/1,080 cells and is therefore operational evidence only. The shared gateway controls endpoint provenance but does not make the underlying models experimentally identical, and the incomplete paired-user coverage cannot support family-level fairness comparisons. API-hosted models and routing may change over time, making exact model-list preflight, returned-model metadata, timestamps, and code/provenance logging essential.

The local output results also expose an evaluation limitation with broader implications. Strict JSON compliance and semantic ranking validity are not interchangeable: Phi required deterministic envelope normalization on 1,439/1,440 cells yet delivered no semantic failures, whereas Qwen was nearly always strict JSON but retained 69 semantic failures. Treating either surface format or post-hoc repair as a proxy for recommendation correctness would therefore distort the evaluation. Candidate-constrained ranking improves auditability but still differs from open-ended conversational recommendation, so these results do not automatically transfer to unrestricted dialogue settings. The secondary analysis adds a complementary warning: small average counterfactual effects can coexist with substantial user-level heterogeneity, and repeat stability can differ materially even when aggregate utility is similar. Fairness auditing should therefore report paired distributions and execution reliability alongside mean utility gaps rather than treating a near-zero average as evidence of universal invariance.

For an IR-for-Good framing, the intended intervention path is concrete: detect preference-irrelevant identity effects with an auditable benchmark; separate them from beneficial personalization; expose model-, domain-, and prompt-specific failure modes; and test mitigations that reduce harmful disparities while preserving relevance. Each link in that mechanism is evaluated rather than assumed.

\section{Conclusion}
FairEval separates ranking sensitivity from preference-conditioned harm and makes output failure part of the measured recommender behavior rather than a preprocessing nuisance. In the complete 2,880-generation local FairSynth study, meaningless identity edits produced no reliable nDCG@10 or Recall@10 shift in either model, and true-versus-shuffled synthetic personality likewise showed no reliable utility gain. Secondary frozen-result analysis shows that these near-zero averages coexist with mixed user-level effects and materially different repeat stability: Phi achieves higher RBO/Jaccard agreement and complete valid repetition coverage, whereas Qwen retains both lower repeat consistency and 69 unrepaired semantic failures. A six-family hosted pilot demonstrated execution feasibility but stopped outcome-blind at 99/1,080 cells, so it is not used for fairness inference. Together, these results validate the controlled-sanity and provenance machinery while showing why fairness audits should combine paired utility consequences, user-level effect distributions, and execution reliability rather than equating either ranking change or clean serialization with fairness.
